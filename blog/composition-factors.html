Composition factors and representation stability

September 1, 2018 · 15 min read
Amin Saied
Member of Technical Staff, Microsoft AI

In 2017, my advisor asked me to compute a family of coefficients cλμc_{\lambda\mu}cλμ​ arising in the study of the Johnson homomorphism of the mapping class group.

Their connection with mapping class groups is not essential to this story. Instead, I want to describe how to compute them efficiently, and how expanding the range of computation revealed hints of a deeper structure among the coefficients.1

Plethysms afoot​

So, briefly, what are the coefficients cλμc_{\lambda\mu}cλμ​?

Recall that, given a partition λ⊢n\lambda\vdash nλ⊢n, the Schur functor Sλ\mathbb S_\lambdaSλ​ assigns to a vector space VVV the image of a Young symmetrizer acting on V⊗nV^{\otimes n}V⊗n. Two familiar examples are the symmetric powers S(n)(V)=Sym⁡n(V)\mathbb S_{(n)}(V)=\operatorname{Sym}^n(V)S(n)​(V)=Symn(V) and exterior powers S(1n)(V)=⋀nV\mathbb S_{(1^n)}(V)=\bigwedge^n VS(1n)​(V)=⋀nV.

The free Lie algebra L(V)\mathcal L(V)L(V) is the Lie algebra generated by VVV, subject only to antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity. It has a natural grading

L(V)=⨁n≥1Ln(V),\mathcal L(V)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}\mathcal L_n(V),L(V)=n≥1⨁​Ln​(V),

where Ln(V)\mathcal L_n(V)Ln​(V) consists of Lie expressions involving exactly nnn inputs from VVV.

We are interested in the coefficients cλμc_{\lambda\mu}cλμ​ defined by the decomposition

Sμ(L(V))≅⨁λSλ(V)⊕cλμ.\mathbb S_\mu\bigl(\mathcal L(V)\bigr) \cong \bigoplus_\lambda \mathbb S_\lambda(V)^{\oplus c_{\lambda\mu}}.Sμ​(L(V))≅λ⨁​Sλ​(V)⊕cλμ​.

In other words, cλμc_{\lambda\mu}cλμ​ is the multiplicity of Sλ(V)\mathbb S_\lambda(V)Sλ​(V) in Sμ(L(V))\mathbb S_\mu(\mathcal L(V))Sμ​(L(V)).2

How might we compute these coefficients?

The first observation is that the graded pieces of the free Lie algebra themselves admit Schur-functor decompositions:

Ln(V)≅⨁ν⊢nSν(V)⊕mν.\mathcal L_n(V)\cong \bigoplus_{\nu\vdash n}\mathbb S_\nu(V)^{\oplus m_\nu}.Ln​(V)≅ν⊢n⨁​Sν​(V)⊕mν​.

Moreover, the multiplicities mνm_\numν​ are well understood. They can be computed using symmetric-group characters, or by counting certain standard Young tableaux.3

So we know how to decompose L(V)\mathcal L(V)L(V). The difficulty comes when we apply another Schur functor Sμ\mathbb S_\muSμ​ to it.

This leads us directly to plethysm.

Plethysm describes the composition of Schur functors. Given partitions μ\muμ and ν\nuν, we seek the decomposition

Sμ(Sν(V))≅⨁λ⊢∣μ∣∣ν∣Sλ(V)⊕pμ,νλ.\mathbb S_\mu\bigl(\mathbb S_\nu(V)\bigr) \cong \bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash|\mu||\nu|} \mathbb S_\lambda(V)^{\oplus p_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}}.Sμ​(Sν​(V))≅λ⊢∣μ∣∣ν∣⨁​Sλ​(V)⊕pμ,νλ​.

The nonnegative integers pμ,νλp_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}pμ,νλ​ are the plethysm coefficients. In the language of symmetric functions, writing sλs_\lambdasλ​ for the Schur function associated with Sλ\mathbb S_\lambdaSλ​, the same calculation is

sμ[sν]=∑λpμ,νλsλ.s_\mu[s_\nu]=\sum_\lambda p_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}s_\lambda.sμ​[sν​]=λ∑​pμ,νλ​sλ​.

For our problem, let Lien=∑ν⊢nmνsν\mathrm{Lie}_n=\sum_{\nu\vdash n}m_\nu s_\nuLien​=∑ν⊢n​mν​sν​ encode the degree-nnn piece of the free Lie algebra. The coefficients we seek are precisely those in the expansion:4

sμ[∑n≥1Lien]=∑λcλμsλ.s_\mu\left[\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Lie}_n\right] =\sum_\lambda c_{\lambda\mu}s_\lambda.sμ​[n≥1∑​Lien​]=λ∑​cλμ​sλ​.

Thus the representation-theoretic question reduces to computing plethysms of symmetric functions.

Unfortunately, plethysm is notoriously difficult.5 Littlewood-Richardson coefficients, which govern tensor products of Schur functors, admit an elegant positive combinatorial rule. No comparably general positive rule is known for plethysm coefficients.6 Even determining which irreducible representations occur in a plethysm can be subtle, and general computations quickly become prohibitively expensive as the partitions grow.

Roll up your sleeves​

Small examples of the coefficients cλμc_{\lambda\mu}cλμ​ can be computed by hand, but the calculations quickly become intractable. Sooner or later, one has to turn to a computer.

A confession: this was the first time in my mathematical career that I had found myself turning to a computer for help. My first attempts were brute force and, unsurprisingly, rather inefficient. They only produced coefficients through degree 5.

There are 18 nonempty partitions through degree 5, giving the 18×1818\times 1818×18 matrix below. Rows index the target partition λ\lambdaλ and columns the source partition μ\muμ.

Two features are immediately clear: the diagonal consists entirely of 1s, and everything above it is zero. Both are simple to prove directly. Since L1(V)=V\mathcal L_1(V)=VL1​(V)=V and there are no degree-zero terms, the lowest-degree part of Sμ(L(V))\mathbb S_\mu(\mathcal L(V))Sμ​(L(V)) is Sμ(V)\mathbb S_\mu(V)Sμ​(V) itself, in degree ∣μ∣|\mu|∣μ∣.

What interests us, then, is what lies strictly below the diagonal. Through degree 5, there is little to suggest a deeper pattern.

The key insight to improve on the naive brute-force approach was to recast the coefficients cλμc_{\lambda\mu}cλμ​ as weighted counts of solutions to certain combinatorial arrangements I called decomposition puzzles. The underlying combinatorics of these puzzles imposed clear restrictions on the kinds of assemblies that are valid, allowing us to significantly reduce the search space.

At the level of degrees, plethysm becomes multiplication and tensor product becomes addition. In the example above, the chosen pairing has degree 2×2+1×1=52\times 2+1\times 1=52×2+1×1=5. Swapping the Lie pieces gives 2×1+1×2=42\times 1+1\times 2=42×1+1×2=4, immediately ruling out that pairing for a degree-5 target.

This cheap check is the starting point of shape analysis.7 By grouping decompositions with the same part sizes, I could perform the search once per shape and reuse the resulting instructions across many calculations. I will give the details below.

This was the algorithm described in my 2017 paper. It extended the computation to degree 14: a 507×507507\times 507507×507 matrix containing 257,049 entries, compared with the original 324.

Visualizing these expanded coefficients revealed a striking block-stability pattern that immediately brought to mind Church, Ellenberg, and Farb's work on FI-modules.8 Blocks in one part of the matrix seemed to recur, with changes, farther down and to the right.

You can explore this observation yourself in the interactive figure below: toggle Show degree boundaries to separate the matrix into blocks of fixed source and target degree. Use the maximum-degree slider to compare the expanded table with the degree-5 calculation.

Before examining that pattern, let us look more closely at the puzzles that made a computation of this size possible.

Decomposition puzzles​

Return to the decomposition of the free Lie algebra, now indexing its irreducible summands individually:

L(V)≅⨁i≥1Sℓi(V).\mathcal L(V)\cong \bigoplus_{i\geq 1}\mathbb S_{\ell_i}(V).L(V)≅i≥1⨁​Sℓi​​(V).

The partitions ℓi\ell_iℓi​ label Lie pieces, with repeated summands listed separately. A μ\muμ-decomposition is a tuple D=(μ1,…,μk)D=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_k)D=(μ1​,…,μk​) of nonempty partitions satisfying ∑j∣μj∣=∣μ∣\sum_j|\mu_j|=|\mu|∑j​∣μj​∣=∣μ∣. Its iterated Littlewood-Richardson coefficient is

αμ(D)=[sμ](sμ1⋯sμk).\alpha_\mu(D) =[s_\mu]\bigl(s_{\mu_1}\cdots s_{\mu_k}\bigr).αμ​(D)=[sμ​](sμ1​​⋯sμk​​).

Retain only the decompositions with αμ(D)>0\alpha_\mu(D)>0αμ​(D)>0. For each one, choose a tuple I=(i1,…,ik)I=(i_1,\ldots,i_k)I=(i1​,…,ik​) of distinct Lie-piece indices and form the assembly

A(D,I)=∏j=1ksμj[sℓij],A(D,I)=\prod_{j=1}^k s_{\mu_j}[s_{\ell_{i_j}}],A(D,I)=j=1∏k​sμj​​[sℓij​​​],

where brackets denote plethysm. The multiplicity of a target partition λ\lambdaλ in this assembly is βλ(D,I)=[sλ]A(D,I)\beta_\lambda(D,I)=[s_\lambda]A(D,I)βλ​(D,I)=[sλ​]A(D,I).

A pairing in which λ\lambdaλ occurs is a solution to the puzzle, with weight αμ(D)βλ(D,I)\alpha_\mu(D)\beta_\lambda(D,I)αμ​(D)βλ​(D,I). Theorem 3.21 of the paper identifies cλμc_{\lambda\mu}cλμ​ with the sum over the set Σ(μ,λ)\Sigma(\mu,\lambda)Σ(μ,λ) of distinct solutions:9

cλμ=∑σ∈Σ(μ,λ)αμ(σ) βλ(σ).c_{\lambda\mu} =\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(\mu,\lambda)} \alpha_\mu(\sigma)\,\beta_\lambda(\sigma).cλμ​=σ∈Σ(μ,λ)∑​αμ​(σ)βλ​(σ).

Shape analysis​

Enumerating every assembly would still require too many expensive plethysms. The useful restriction comes from homogeneity: an assembly has degree ∑j∣μj∣ ∣ℓij∣\sum_j|\mu_j|\,|\ell_{i_j}|∑j​∣μj​∣∣ℓij​​∣, so it can contribute to λ⊢d\lambda\vdash dλ⊢d only when that sum equals ddd.

This condition depends on the decomposition only through its shape

θ=(∣μ1∣,…,∣μk∣),\theta=(|\mu_1|,\ldots,|\mu_k|),θ=(∣μ1​∣,…,∣μk​∣),

with the pieces ordered by decreasing size. Writing ri=∣ℓi∣r_i=|\ell_i|ri​=∣ℓi​∣ for the Lie-piece degrees, the condition becomes

θ1ri1+⋯+θkrik=d.\theta_1r_{i_1}+\cdots+\theta_kr_{i_k}=d.θ1​ri1​​+⋯+θk​rik​​=d.

The shape also gives an upper bound on the degrees of the Lie pieces we need to consider.10 The algorithm searches this smaller collection for degree-compatible pairings and caches their indices. These instructions can then be reused by every decomposition of the same shape.

For θ=(2,1)\theta=(2,1)θ=(2,1) and target degree 5, the ten available Lie pieces give 90 ordered pairs. The bound restricts us to pieces of degree at most 3, leaving six pairs. The equation 2ri1+ri2=52r_{i_1}+r_{i_2}=52ri1​​+ri2​​=5 then leaves just two: the degree pairs (1,3)(1,3)(1,3) and (2,1)(2,1)(2,1).

The instruction count is not itself a coefficient. For μ=(2,1)\mu=(2,1)μ=(2,1) and D=((2),(1))D=((2),(1))D=((2),(1)), each of these two assemblies contributes one copy of the target (3,2)(3,2)(3,2). The decomposition D′=((1,1),(1))D'=((1,1),(1))D′=((1,1),(1)) contributes one further copy, so the full weighted sum is

c(3,2),(2,1)=3.c_{(3,2),(2,1)}=3.c(3,2),(2,1)​=3.

The algorithm and its computational range​

For a fixed target degree ddd, the implementation computes all coefficients with ∣λ∣=d|\lambda|=d∣λ∣=d simultaneously:

  1. Construct the instruction lookup for each shape and target degree.
  2. Enumerate the decompositions with nonzero Littlewood-Richardson multiplicity.
  3. Assemble the compatible instructions and accumulate their Schur coefficients with the appropriate weights and overcount correction.

The optimization does not eliminate plethysm; it avoids evaluating assemblies that cannot contribute.

Diagonal stabilization​

Return now to the pattern in the expanded matrix. The similarities between successive (m,d)(m,d)(m,d)- and (m+1,d+1)(m+1,d+1)(m+1,d+1)-blocks, where mmm and ddd are the source and target degrees, suggest adding a box to the first row of each indexing partition. Both degrees increase by one, while their difference stays fixed.

Write λ+r=(λ1+r,λ2,…)\lambda^{+r}=(\lambda_1+r,\lambda_2,\ldots)λ+r=(λ1​+r,λ2​,…) for adding rrr boxes to the first row. For a fixed pair (λ,μ)(\lambda,\mu)(λ,μ), consider the sequence

cλμ,cλ+1μ+1,cλ+2μ+2,…c_{\lambda\mu},\quad c_{\lambda^{+1}\mu^{+1}},\quad c_{\lambda^{+2}\mu^{+2}},\quad\ldotscλμ​,cλ+1μ+1​,cλ+2μ+2​,…

For example, the pair λ=(2,2,1)\lambda=(2,2,1)λ=(2,2,1), μ=(1,1)\mu=(1,1)μ=(1,1) gives the initial values 2,11,142,11,142,11,14, followed by the value 14 through r=9r=9r=9, the endpoint of the degree-14 computation. The figure allows this sequence to be compared with other pairs, including any pair selected in the matrix.

The computed plateaus suggest eventual constancy along these diagonals, rather than merely a similarity between finite blocks.

Conjecture 5.4 of the paper states that, for every fixed pair λ,μ\lambda,\muλ,μ, there exist constants N,CN,CN,C such that

cλ+rμ+r=Cfor all r≥N.c_{\lambda^{+r}\mu^{+r}}=C \qquad\text{for all }r\geq N.cλ+rμ+r​=Cfor all r≥N.

The finite computation motivates this statement but does not prove it or determine the onset of permanent stabilization.

Representation stability via PD-modules​

Chapter 5 of the thesis introduces PD-modules to describe this simultaneous growth. They organize representations indexed by two finite sets, with compatible maps that enlarge both sets by the same amount.12 Schur-Weyl duality identifies our coefficients as multiplicities in a PD-module built from the free Lie algebra.

The key condition is finite generation in rank: for each fixed difference between the two degrees, finitely many generators suffice under these diagonal structure maps. Theorem 5.5.3 shows that PD-modules with this property satisfy representation stability.

For the free Lie algebra, finite generation comes from the same degree constraint used by the algorithm. At fixed rank q=∣λ∣−∣μ∣q=|\lambda|-|\mu|q=∣λ∣−∣μ∣, it gives

q=∑j∣μj∣(∣ℓij∣−1).q=\sum_j|\mu_j|\bigl(|\ell_{i_j}|-1\bigr).q=j∑​∣μj​∣(∣ℓij​​∣−1).

Every Lie piece of degree greater than one contributes at least ∣μj∣|\mu_j|∣μj​∣ to this sum. The total source degree assigned to such pieces is therefore at most qqq. All unbounded growth must occur in the factor paired with the unique degree-one Lie piece, L1(V)=V\mathcal L_1(V)=VL1​(V)=V. This growth is encoded by the PD structure maps; the remaining assemblies have bounded degree and supply finitely many generators. Theorem 5.6.4 makes this argument precise.

It follows that our coefficients stabilize under simultaneous first-row augmentation. This is Theorem 5.6.5 of the thesis, proving the conjecture above.

The computation supplied evidence for the stability statement. The PD-module structure explains why it holds beyond the computed range.
