In 2017, my advisor asked me to compute a family of coefficients
$c_{\lambda\mu}$ arising in the study of the Johnson homomorphism of the
mapping class group.

Their connection with mapping class groups is not essential to this story.
Instead, I want to describe how to compute them efficiently, and how
expanding the range of computation revealed hints of a deeper structure
among the coefficients.[^thesis]

## Plethysms afoot

So, briefly, what are the coefficients $c_{\lambda\mu}$?

Recall that, given a partition $\lambda\vdash n$, the **Schur functor**
$\mathbb S_\lambda$ assigns to a vector space $V$ the image of a Young symmetrizer acting on
$V^{\otimes n}$. Two familiar examples are the symmetric powers
$\mathbb S_{(n)}(V)=\operatorname{Sym}^n(V)$ and exterior powers
$\mathbb S_{(1^n)}(V)=\bigwedge^n V$.

The **free Lie algebra** $\mathcal L(V)$ is the Lie algebra generated by
$V$, subject only to antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity. It has a natural
grading

$$
\mathcal L(V)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}\mathcal L_n(V),
$$

where $\mathcal L_n(V)$ consists of Lie expressions involving exactly
$n$ inputs from $V$.

We are interested in the coefficients $c_{\lambda\mu}$ defined by the
decomposition

$$
\mathbb S_\mu\bigl(\mathcal L(V)\bigr)
\cong
\bigoplus_\lambda
\mathbb S_\lambda(V)^{\oplus c_{\lambda\mu}}.
$$

In other words, $c_{\lambda\mu}$ is the multiplicity of
$\mathbb S_\lambda(V)$ in $\mathbb S_\mu(\mathcal L(V))$.[^conventions]

How might we compute these coefficients?

The first observation is that the graded pieces of the free Lie algebra
themselves admit Schur-functor decompositions:

$$
\mathcal L_n(V)\cong
\bigoplus_{\nu\vdash n}\mathbb S_\nu(V)^{\oplus m_\nu}.
$$

Moreover, the multiplicities $m_\nu$ are well understood. They can be
computed using symmetric-group characters, or by counting certain
standard Young tableaux.[^lie-multiplicities]

So we know how to decompose $\mathcal L(V)$. The difficulty comes when
we apply another Schur functor $\mathbb S_\mu$ to it.

This leads us directly to **plethysm**.

Plethysm describes the composition of Schur functors. Given partitions
$\mu$ and $\nu$, we seek the decomposition

$$
\mathbb S_\mu\bigl(\mathbb S_\nu(V)\bigr)
\cong
\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash|\mu||\nu|}
\mathbb S_\lambda(V)^{\oplus p_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}}.
$$

The nonnegative integers $p_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}$ are the **plethysm
coefficients**. In the language of symmetric functions, writing
$s_\lambda$ for the Schur function associated with $\mathbb S_\lambda$,
the same calculation is

$$
s_\mu[s_\nu]=\sum_\lambda p_{\mu,\nu}^{\lambda}s_\lambda.
$$

For our problem, let $\mathrm{Lie}_n=\sum_{\nu\vdash n}m_\nu s_\nu$
encode the degree-$n$ piece of the free Lie algebra. The coefficients
we seek are precisely those in the expansion:[^lie-series]

$$
s_\mu\left[\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Lie}_n\right]
=\sum_\lambda c_{\lambda\mu}s_\lambda.
$$

Thus the representation-theoretic question reduces to computing plethysms
of symmetric functions.

Unfortunately, **plethysm is notoriously difficult**.[^plethysm-complexity] Littlewood-Richardson
coefficients, which govern tensor products of Schur functors, admit an
elegant positive combinatorial rule. No comparably general positive rule
is known for plethysm coefficients.[^plethysm-combinatorics] Even determining which irreducible
representations occur in a plethysm can be subtle, and general
computations quickly become prohibitively expensive as the partitions grow.

## Roll up your sleeves

Small examples of the coefficients $c_{\lambda\mu}$ can be computed by
hand, but the calculations quickly become intractable. Sooner or later,
one has to turn to a computer.

A confession: this was the first time in my mathematical career that I
had found myself turning to a computer for help. My first attempts were
brute force and, unsurprisingly, rather inefficient. They only produced
coefficients through degree 5.

There are 18 nonempty partitions through degree 5, giving the
$18\times 18$ matrix below. Rows index the target partition $\lambda$
and columns the source partition $\mu$.

<DegreeFiveMatrix />

Two features are immediately clear: the diagonal consists entirely of
1s, and everything above it is zero. Both are simple to prove directly.
Since $\mathcal L_1(V)=V$ and there are no degree-zero terms, the
lowest-degree part of $\mathbb S_\mu(\mathcal L(V))$ is
$\mathbb S_\mu(V)$ itself, in degree $|\mu|$.

What interests us, then, is what lies **strictly below the diagonal**.
Through degree 5, there is little to suggest a deeper pattern.

The key insight to improve on the naive brute-force approach was to recast
the coefficients $c_{\lambda\mu}$ as weighted counts of solutions to
certain combinatorial arrangements I called **decomposition puzzles**.
The underlying combinatorics of these puzzles imposed clear restrictions
on the kinds of assemblies that are valid, allowing us to significantly
reduce the search space.

The pairing from [Example 3.18 of the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.04326#page=10)
contributes 1 to $c_{(3,2),(2,1)}$.

</DecompositionPuzzleCartoon>

At the level of **degrees**, plethysm becomes multiplication and tensor
product becomes addition. In the example above, the chosen pairing has
degree $2\times 2+1\times 1=5$. Swapping the Lie pieces gives
$2\times 1+1\times 2=4$, immediately ruling out that pairing for a
degree-5 target.

This cheap check is the starting point of **shape analysis**.[^shape-arithmetic]
By grouping decompositions with the same part sizes, I could perform the
search once per shape and reuse the resulting instructions across many
calculations. I will give the details below.

This was the algorithm described in my
[2017 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04326). It extended the computation
to degree 14: a $507\times 507$ matrix containing **257,049 entries**,
compared with the original 324.

Visualizing these expanded coefficients revealed a striking block-stability
pattern that immediately brought to mind Church, Ellenberg, and Farb's
work on FI-modules.[^fi-modules] Blocks in one part of the matrix seemed
to recur, with changes, farther down and to the right.

## Decomposition puzzles

Return to the decomposition of the free Lie algebra, now indexing its
irreducible summands individually:

$$
\mathcal L(V)\cong
\bigoplus_{i\geq 1}\mathbb S_{\ell_i}(V).
$$

The partitions $\ell_i$ label **Lie pieces**, with repeated summands
listed separately. A $\mu$-decomposition is a tuple
$D=(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_k)$ of nonempty partitions satisfying
$\sum_j|\mu_j|=|\mu|$. Its iterated Littlewood-Richardson coefficient is

$$
\alpha_\mu(D)
=[s_\mu]\bigl(s_{\mu_1}\cdots s_{\mu_k}\bigr).
$$

Retain only the decompositions with $\alpha_\mu(D)>0$. For each one,
choose a tuple $I=(i_1,\ldots,i_k)$ of distinct Lie-piece indices and
form the **assembly**

$$
A(D,I)=\prod_{j=1}^k s_{\mu_j}[s_{\ell_{i_j}}],
$$

where brackets denote plethysm. The multiplicity of a target partition
$\lambda$ in this assembly is
$\beta_\lambda(D,I)=[s_\lambda]A(D,I)$.

A pairing in which $\lambda$ occurs is a **solution** to the puzzle,
with weight $\alpha_\mu(D)\beta_\lambda(D,I)$. Theorem 3.21 of the
paper identifies $c_{\lambda\mu}$ with the sum over the set
$\Sigma(\mu,\lambda)$ of distinct solutions:[^puzzle-weights]

$$
c_{\lambda\mu}
=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma(\mu,\lambda)}
\alpha_\mu(\sigma)\,\beta_\lambda(\sigma).
$$

## Shape analysis

Enumerating every assembly would still require too many expensive
plethysms. The useful restriction comes from homogeneity: an assembly has
degree $\sum_j|\mu_j|\,|\ell_{i_j}|$, so it can contribute to
$\lambda\vdash d$ only when that sum equals $d$.

This condition depends on the decomposition only through its **shape**

$$
\theta=(|\mu_1|,\ldots,|\mu_k|),
$$

with the pieces ordered by decreasing size. Writing $r_i=|\ell_i|$
for the Lie-piece degrees, the condition becomes

$$
\theta_1r_{i_1}+\cdots+\theta_kr_{i_k}=d.
$$

The shape also gives an upper bound on the degrees of the Lie pieces
we need to consider.[^degree-bound] The algorithm searches this smaller
collection for degree-compatible pairings and caches their indices.
These **instructions** can then be reused by every decomposition of
the same shape.

<ShapeAnalysis />

For $\theta=(2,1)$ and target degree 5, the ten available Lie pieces
give 90 ordered pairs. The bound restricts us to pieces of degree at
most 3, leaving six pairs. The equation $2r_{i_1}+r_{i_2}=5$ then leaves
just two: the degree pairs $(1,3)$ and $(2,1)$.

The instruction count is not itself a coefficient. For
$\mu=(2,1)$ and $D=((2),(1))$, each of these two assemblies contributes
one copy of the target $(3,2)$. The decomposition
$D'=((1,1),(1))$ contributes one further copy, so the full weighted sum is

$$
c_{(3,2),(2,1)}=3.
$$

## The algorithm and its computational range

For a fixed target degree $d$, the implementation computes all coefficients
with $|\lambda|=d$ simultaneously:

1. Construct the instruction lookup for each shape and target degree.
2. Enumerate the decompositions with nonzero Littlewood-Richardson
   multiplicity.
3. Assemble the compatible instructions and accumulate their Schur
   coefficients with the appropriate weights and overcount correction.

The optimization does not eliminate plethysm; it avoids evaluating
assemblies that cannot contribute.

The following comparison is from the experiments reported in the paper:

| Quantity | Direct baseline | Decomposition-puzzle algorithm |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Largest degree reached in the paper | 5 | 14 |
| Entries in the resulting full matrix | 324 | 257,049 |
| Time to compute through degree 5 | 119 seconds | 0.239 seconds |

The paper's **"over 750-fold"** claim refers to the expanded number of
matrix entries. The degree-5 timing comparison is a separate result:
roughly 498 times faster in that experiment.

These are timings from the 2017 experiments, not benchmarks of current
versions of Sage.[^timings]

## Diagonal stabilization

Return now to the pattern in the expanded matrix. The similarities
between successive $(m,d)$- and $(m+1,d+1)$-blocks, where $m$ and $d$
are the source and target degrees, suggest adding a box
to the first row of each indexing partition. Both degrees increase by
one, while their difference stays fixed.

Write $\lambda^{+r}=(\lambda_1+r,\lambda_2,\ldots)$ for adding $r$ boxes
to the first row. For a fixed pair $(\lambda,\mu)$, consider the sequence

$$
c_{\lambda\mu},\quad
c_{\lambda^{+1}\mu^{+1}},\quad
c_{\lambda^{+2}\mu^{+2}},\quad\ldots
$$

For example, the pair $\lambda=(2,2,1)$, $\mu=(1,1)$ gives the initial
values $2,11,14$, followed by the value 14 through $r=9$, the endpoint
of the degree-14 computation. The figure allows this sequence to be
compared with other pairs, including any pair selected in the matrix.

<StabilityExplorer />

The computed plateaus suggest eventual constancy along these diagonals,
rather than merely a similarity between finite blocks.

Conjecture 5.4 of the paper states that, for every fixed pair
$\lambda,\mu$, there exist constants $N,C$ such that

$$
c_{\lambda^{+r}\mu^{+r}}=C
\qquad\text{for all }r\geq N.
$$

The finite computation motivates this statement but does not prove it or
determine the onset of permanent stabilization.

## Representation stability via PD-modules

Chapter 5 of the thesis introduces **PD-modules** to describe this
simultaneous growth. They organize representations indexed by two finite
sets, with compatible maps that enlarge both sets by the same
amount.[^pd-modules] Schur-Weyl duality identifies our coefficients as
multiplicities in a PD-module built from the free Lie algebra.

The key condition is **finite generation in rank**: for each fixed
difference between the two degrees, finitely many generators suffice
under these diagonal structure maps. Theorem 5.5.3 shows that PD-modules
with this property satisfy representation stability.

For the free Lie algebra, finite generation comes from the same degree
constraint used by the algorithm. At fixed rank
$q=|\lambda|-|\mu|$, it gives

$$
q=\sum_j|\mu_j|\bigl(|\ell_{i_j}|-1\bigr).
$$

Every Lie piece of degree greater than one contributes at least
$|\mu_j|$ to this sum. The total source degree assigned to such pieces
is therefore at most $q$. All unbounded growth must occur in the factor
paired with the unique degree-one Lie piece, $\mathcal L_1(V)=V$.
This growth is encoded by the PD structure maps; the remaining
assemblies have bounded degree and supply finitely many generators.
Theorem 5.6.4 makes this argument precise.

It follows that our coefficients stabilize under simultaneous first-row
augmentation. This is **Theorem 5.6.5** of the thesis, proving the
conjecture above.

The computation supplied evidence for the stability statement. The
PD-module structure explains why it holds beyond the computed range.

[^thesis]: Unpacking that structure turned out to be at the heart of my thesis.

[^conventions]: Work over a field $\mathbf{k}$ of characteristic zero.
    These are universal Schur-functor multiplicities: interpret the identities
    degree by degree, or evaluate on $V$ of sufficiently large dimension in
    each degree. For nonempty $\mu$, the decomposition extends over
    arbitrarily large degrees, but only finitely many partitions $\lambda$
    contribute in any given degree.

[^lie-multiplicities]: For $\nu\vdash n$, the multiplicity $m_\nu$ counts
    standard Young tableaux of shape $\nu$ whose major index is congruent
    to 1 modulo $n$. The major index is the sum of entries $i$ for which
    $i+1$ appears in a lower row. See
    [Theorem 3.4 of the paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.04326#page=6),
    following Stanley's *Enumerative Combinatorics*, Volume 2.

[^lie-series]: Explicitly,

    $$
    \mathrm{Lie}_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}
    \mu_{\mathrm{Mob}}(d)\,p_d^{n/d},
    $$

    where $\mu_{\mathrm{Mob}}$ is the Mobius function and $p_d$ is the
    degree-$d$ power-sum symmetric function. The infinite sum is taken in
    the degree-completed ring of symmetric functions; computing through
    any fixed degree requires only finitely many homogeneous terms.

[^plethysm-complexity]: Nick Fischer and Christian Ikenmeyer,
    [*The Computational Complexity of Plethysm Coefficients*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.00788),
    *Computational Complexity* 29 (2020), Article 8, prove that computing
    plethysm coefficients is #P-hard and deciding positivity is NP-hard.
    Both results already hold for the symmetric-power family
    $s_{(n)}[s_{(m)}]$ with any fixed $m\geq 3$. In this same family,
    fixing the outer parameter $n$ permits polynomial-time computation;
    see Theorems 3.1-3.2 and Proposition 3.3.

[^plethysm-combinatorics]: Richard P. Stanley poses the combinatorial
    interpretation problem as Problem 9 in
    [*Positivity Problems and Conjectures in Algebraic Combinatorics*](https://math.mit.edu/~rstan/pubs/pubfiles/116.pdf),
    in *Mathematics: Frontiers and Perspectives* (AMS, 2000), pp. 295-319.
    For a modern account of the open problem and computational techniques,
    see Laura Colmenarejo, Rosa Orellana, Franco Saliola, Anne Schilling,
    and Mike Zabrocki,
    [*The Mystery of Plethysm Coefficients*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07258),
    in *Open Problems in Algebraic Combinatorics*, *Proceedings of Symposia
    in Pure Mathematics* 110 (2024), pp. 275-292, especially Problem 1.1.

[^fi-modules]: Thomas Church, Jordan S. Ellenberg, and Benson Farb,
    [*FI-modules and stability for representations of symmetric groups*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.4533),
    *Duke Mathematical Journal* 164, no. 9 (2015), pp. 1833-1910.

[^puzzle-weights]: The weighted formula follows by iterating the
    Littlewood-Richardson rule for Schur functors of direct sums.
    Distinct solutions are taken modulo simultaneous permutation of the
    paired factors. The implementation fixes a representative decomposition
    and enumerates ordered instructions. If its distinct partitions occur
    $n_1,\ldots,n_t$ times, it corrects the overcount by
    $1/(n_1!\cdots n_t!)$. Repeated Lie pieces at distinct indices remain
    distinct summands.

[^shape-arithmetic]: See [Proposition 3.24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.04326#page=12)
    and [Figure 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.04326#page=13) of the paper.
    Matching the target degree is a necessary condition; the subsequent
    assembly determines which target partitions occur and with what multiplicity.

[^degree-bound]: Order the shape parts decreasingly and the Lie-piece
    degrees $r_1,r_2,\ldots$ increasingly, counting multiplicities.
    Lemma 3.28 of the paper gives the upper bound

    $$
    \varphi(\theta,d)=
    \left\lfloor
    \frac{d-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\theta_jr_j}{\theta_k}
    \right\rfloor.
    $$

    Pairing the largest shape parts with the smallest available degrees
    leaves the greatest possible allowance for the remaining piece.

[^timings]: Averages of ten runs on a 2.3 GHz Intel Core i7 with 16 GB
    of RAM, as reported in the paper.

[^pd-modules]: Formally, a PD-module is a functor
    $\mathsf{PD}\to\operatorname{Vect}_{\mathbf{k}}$. A morphism between
    pairs of finite sets consists of an injection in each coordinate and
    a bijection between their complements. Its values in bidegree $(i,n)$
    are $(S_i,S_n)$-bimodules; the structure maps preserve the rank $n-i$.
    Sections 5.5 and 5.6 of the thesis give the stability theorem and the
    free-Lie construction.

[^reconstruction]: The exporter, `scripts/generate-composition-data.py`,
    uses degree-truncated power-sum plethysm with exact rational arithmetic.
    It checks character orthogonality, integrality, nonnegativity, the
    equal-degree identity, and examples from the original code. This is
    not a port or timing comparison of the original algorithm.
